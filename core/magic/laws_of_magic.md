# The Laws of Magic

Every spell, ritual, miracle, and minor working in Ressam operates beneath a small set of laws. They are not rules written by mortals, and they are not doctrine — they are the physics of magic itself, as inviolable as gravity and as indifferent to intent. Scholars of Mafsoleios call them the Constants. The Fe Alada calls them the Design. Hedge wizards, when they discover them the hard way, tend to call them something unprintable.

This document exists for one purpose: when the spell lists are silent, the Laws speak. Any effect a caster attempts — however clever, however novel — can be measured against them. If it violates a Law, it does not happen. Not "it fails," not "it costs more." It does not happen, in the same way a stone does not fall upward. Everything else is merely a question of price.

## The Five Laws

### **I. The Law of Borrowed Substance**

*Nothing is ever made. Everything is borrowed.*

Magic does not create. It condenses — gathering energy and pressing it, temporarily, into the shape of matter. Conjured stone is real stone while the magic holds it. Conjured water quenches real thirst. But condensed substance is a loan, and the world always collects. Left unattended, conjured matter slows, thins, and unravels back into the energy it was borrowed from.

This is why no mage has ever conjured a fortune, fed a famine, or raised a fortress from nothing. The gold fades from the merchant's palm. The bread turns to warmth and absence in the belly. The tower stands only as long as its maker keeps paying for it.

**The anchor.** The loan can be stretched, never forgiven. Magic bound into a physical vessel — worked into steel, fired into clay, woven into cloth — decays at the pace of its anchor rather than the pace of a caster's attention. This is the entire discipline of enchantment: not creation, but exceptionally patient borrowing. An enchanted blade still dies. It simply dies in millennia rather than hours, its magic thinning generation by generation until some distant heir wonders why grandfather's sword no longer sings.

### **II. The Law of the Present**

*Magic touches what is. Never what was. Never what will be.*

Time admits no hand upon it. No magic reverses a moment, halts one, or reaches ahead to one that has not yet arrived. Divination — true divination — is the art of reading the present with impossible thoroughness: what exists now, what is written now, what is remembered now, what moves through the world at this very instant. A master diviner can seem to know the future, the way a master physician can seem to prophesy a death. Both are only reading the present with terrible clarity.

The unrecorded past is equally sealed. What no mind remembers and no surface bears witness to is gone beyond all reaching. History that leaves no trace leaves nothing for magic to find.

**The dispensation.** The gods stand outside the river and see its whole length. When prophecy enters the world, it enters as a gift — a vision granted, never a spell cast. No mortal commands it, no formula produces it, and no recipient should trust it plainly: divine sight arrives in symbol and fragment, shaped by a mind that does not think in mortal sequence. Prophecy is real in Ressam. It is also never, ever *available*. The difference matters.

### **III. The Law of the Soul**

*Magic cannot create a soul, destroy a soul, or grow a soul.*

The soul is the one substance in creation that magic did not make and cannot unmake. It can be touched — held, moved, pinned, imprisoned — for the soul already exists, and magic may handle what exists. But no working ever kindled a new soul, snuffed one from being, or caused one to become more than it was. Growth belongs to life alone. A soul changes by living, and living is precisely the thing no spell provides.

This is why necromancy, for all its horror, is a craft of *bodies*. The corpse walks; the person does not return. An empire once animated the dead at the scale of a continent, night after night for centuries, and in all that time not one soul came back through the door. The door does not open from this side.

**The pinning.** Mafsoleios found the edge of this Law and built upon it. Their practice does not restore the departed — nothing does. It *pins*: catching a soul in the moment before its final leaving and fastening it back into its flesh, the way one presses a flower before it can wilt. The result is exactly what the Law predicts. The pinned live on as they were at the instant of death — same loves, same grudges, same half-finished thoughts — and never an inch further. They do not grow. They do not learn, not truly. They are a portrait that answers when spoken to. Mafsoleios calls this preservation. Whether the pinned would call it living is a question its scholars have learned not to ask aloud.

Above this Law, as above all of them, stand the gods. A soul burst back into primordial energy is beyond every mortal art — and remains, to a god, merely scattered. Miracles of true restoration exist. They are miracles precisely because nothing beneath heaven can perform them.

### **IV. The Law of Essential Nature**

*Mortal craft alters what a thing does. Only will alters what a thing is.*

Everything in creation has a nature — the truth of what it is beneath every circumstance. Spellcraft, arcane or divine, works upon *state*: it heats the stone but cannot make it cease to be stone, enrages the man but cannot make him a different man, disguises the wolf but leaves the wolf entire beneath the seeming. Every transformation wrought by formula or prayer is a veneer stretched over the true form, and veneers do not hold. The nature beneath always, eventually, reasserts itself.

**The exception is older than the rule's students.** Some beings were not born but *willed* — dreamed, spoken, or wanted into existence by powers that did not consult nature when they made them. The Mystik races are the exception given flesh: their very being is an act of nature overwritten, and what was made by will may, in some measure, will in turn. The ancient magic of the Dzinari touched identity itself — not the veneer, but the thing beneath — and the school of Subjugation is the fragment of that art that survived into mortal hands. This is why Subjugation feels wrong in a way no other school does, and why those who denounce it as a violation are not being superstitious. It is not a tenth school of magic. It is a leak from a different order of being, worked by practitioners who do not understand what they are holding.

For everyone and everything else, the Law is absolute. The wise treat apparent exceptions as exactly what they are: evidence that something in the room was never mortal to begin with.

### **V. The Law of Connection**

*Magic requires a thread. No thread, no working.*

Power does not cross the world unaided. To affect a thing, a caster must be connected to it — by sight, by touch, or by sympathy. Sight is the common thread: what the eye holds, the will can reach. Touch is the intimate one. Sympathy is the third and most dangerous: a part standing for the whole. Blood, hair, a possession worn against the skin for years — things that were *of* someone remain faintly *joined* to them, and a skilled practitioner can work along that joining.

There is no fourth thread. A name is not a connection; a description is not a connection; hatred, however pure, is not a connection. No caster steps from one place to another without crossing the distance between, and no seer finds a stranger by wishing hard enough. This is why the careful burn their cut hair, why the powerful are buried in secret, and why the truly paranoid are right to be. To be scried upon or struck from afar, something of you must first be *taken* — which means the theft, not the spell, is where such stories truly begin.

## The Three Costs

Beneath the Five Laws sit three principles of price. Where the Laws say *never*, the Costs say *only ever at a price* — and the price cannot be haggled down, only paid in a different coin.

### **Conservation**

Magic redirects far more easily than it imposes. To fan an existing flame, deepen an existing shadow, hasten a wound the body is already closing — this is working *with* the world, and the world half-cooperates. To conjure fire in the rain, light in the deep dark, vigor in the dying — this is working *against* the world, and the world resists with its whole weight. The further an effect stands from what nature was already doing, the more it demands of the one who forces it. A practitioner who understands this reads a battlefield, a sickroom, or a storm the way a sailor reads wind: not as scenery, but as the difference between an easy working and a ruinous one.

**Healing lives here, and lives humbly.** The body heals; magic does not. What healing magic provides is the tourniquet, never the mending — flesh borrowed and pressed over the wound, blood commanded to slow, a dying man's thread held taut long enough to matter. It is condensed substance like any other, and the First Law collects on it like any other: the borrowed flesh fades, and beneath it either the body has done its own slow work, or the wound remains. Magic buys time. Only rest, food, and the stubbornness of living tissue ever bought recovery.

### **Signature**

All magic announces itself. It sounds, it shines, it stains. Every working leaves residue hanging in the place it happened — a texture in the air that trained senses can read for hours after, telling the schooled investigator what was done, roughly when, and by what manner of art. There are no untraceable murders by spell. There are only investigators who did not arrive in time, or did not know how to look.

**Shadowmancy is the contest, not the exception.** The school of shadow exists to muffle — to dim the sound, soften the shine, thin the stain. It is very good at this. It is not perfect, because it cannot be: the Law beneath the Cost is that *magic cannot wholly hide magic*, for the hiding is itself a working and leaves its own mark. A place scrubbed by shadow reads as exactly that — a silence too complete, a cleanness where residue ought to be. To the untrained it is nothing. To one who knows the craft, an absence with edges is a signature like any other, and only one school in the world signs its work that way.

### **Reciprocity**

Arcane magic pays in advance — in years of study, in materials, in the merciless precision of formulae that do not forgive error. Divine magic pays *forever*. Power granted by a god arrives with the god attached: their judgment, their doctrine, their memory of every petition and what it was spent on. A miracle is not a tool but a favour, and favour is a ledger. The faithful who drift from their deity's nature find their petitions answered slowly, then reluctantly, then not at all — not as punishment, exactly, but as the simple truth that one cannot draw from a well after walking away from it. There is no divine magic without relationship, and no relationship without obligation running in both directions.

## Adjudication: When the Books Are Silent

The spell lists cannot anticipate every intention a caster will ever have. The Laws can. When something novel is attempted, walk it through in order:

**First — does it cross a Law?** If the attempt creates permanent substance from nothing, touches past or future, kindles or grows a soul, rewrites essential nature by mortal craft, or reaches a target with no thread of connection — then it simply cannot happen. Do not soften this into "very difficult." The Laws are not high walls; they are the shape of the world. A caster attempting the impossible achieves nothing, learns something, and perhaps wastes what they spent finding out. Holding this line without exception is what makes every other ruling trustworthy: players can only plan around walls that never move.

**Second — is it small?** If the effect is brief, humble, and thematic to the caster's art, it belongs to the realm of minor workings — the everyday texture of a practitioner's life, priced accordingly.

**Third — then it is a spell, and spells are priced, never refused.** Anything lawful but substantial should be measured like any other spell: what does it do, what does it touch, how is it delivered — then weighed against the Three Costs. Is it working with the world or against it? How loudly will it announce itself? Whose favour, if anyone's, is being drawn upon? The answer to a lawful-but-ambitious attempt is never "no." It is a price — and the caster's honest reckoning with whether they can pay it.

Held to consistently, these three questions make magic in Ressam feel less like a menu and more like a science half-understood: bounded, discoverable, and dangerous chiefly to those who mistake its silences for permission.
